The Law of the River: Paper Water vs. Wet Water

How the 1922 Compact's overestimate of river flow, the senior priority system, and two decades of reservoir drawdowns led to the post-2026 negotiations.

1. The 1922 Compact Baseline

Historic Foundation

Negotiators assumed the river produced over 17 MAF per year, creating an immediate gap between legal promises and physical supply.

Delegates from the seven basin states and Herbert Hoover signed the 1922 Colorado River Compact in Santa Fe, New Mexico, splitting the watershed at Lees Ferry, Arizona. The compact apportioned 7.5 million acre-feet (MAF) per year to the Upper Basin and 7.5 MAF per year to the Lower Basin in perpetuity (Art. III.a), plus authorized an additional 1.0 MAF of Lower Basin consumptive use (Art. III.b). In 1944, the Mexican Water Treaty committed another 1.5 MAF per year at Morelos Dam (Art. 10), bringing statutory paper water apportionments to 17.5 MAF per year—without setting aside water for reservoir evaporation and transit losses (~1.60 MAF/yr, totaling 19.10 MAF/yr).

Baseline Hydrology: Paper Allocations vs. Physical Wet Water

Negotiators based their allocations on gage records from the unusually wet period between 1905 and 1922, estimating average annual river runoff above 17 MAF. Early USGS field warnings from hydrologist E.C. LaRue showed that long-term flows were significantly lower, but commissioners prioritized state unanimity over hydrology. A century of USGS records and tree-ring data show that natural inflow averages closer to 12.50 MAF per year (2000–2026). Because reservoir evaporation and transit losses consume another ~1.60 MAF per year, total basin demand reaches 19.10 MAF/yr, creating a 6.60 MAF/yr structural overdraft against physical inflows.

2. Senior Rights and Priority Cuts

Legal Hierarchy

The doctrine of prior appropriation protects older agricultural claims while junior municipal canals take early cuts during shortages.

Western water administration follows the doctrine of prior appropriation: "first in time, first in right." The user who first put river water to beneficial use holds senior legal priority and receives a full allocation before newer rights receive water. In the Colorado River Basin, this rule created an unequal priority structure that determines which states absorb shortages first:

Senior Present Perfected Rights in California (1901): Southern California agricultural users—led by the Imperial Irrigation District (IID) and Coachella Valley Water District—hold senior water rights established in 1901 that pre-date the 1922 Compact. Confirmed by the Supreme Court in Arizona v. California (1963), IID's 3.1 MAF entitlement is senior to all municipal water projects across the Lower Basin.
Subordination of the Central Arizona Project (1968): To obtain federal authorization and funding for the 336-mile Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal in the 1968 Basin Project Act, Arizona agreed that CAP deliveries are junior to California's 4.4 MAF baseline allocation. As a result, when shortage triggers occur, Arizona takes the first and deepest cuts while senior California agricultural users receive their full supply.
Upper Basin Headwater Administration: Without large downstream regulatory reservoirs above their diversion points, Upper Basin users divert water directly from mountain streams based on local priority calls. Senior calls at headwater facilities regularly curtail junior Front Range trans-mountain diversions during late-summer low flows. Because of these physical and legal limits, Upper Basin consumptive use averages 4.0 to 4.5 MAF per year, well below its 7.5 MAF paper apportionment.

3. Draining Storage to Cover the Deficit

Storage Depletion

The basin covered a multi-year gap between high demand and falling snowmelt by drawing down 37 MAF of stored reservoir water.

Even when Upper Basin snowmelt dropped after 2000, Lower Basin users continued drawing full allocations while reservoir evaporation and transit losses consumed roughly 1.5 MAF per year. Instead of reducing water use to match lower snowmelt, the basin operated at an annual structural deficit of 2.0 to 3.5 MAF per year.

The basin bridged this multi-year deficit by draining Lake Powell and Lake Mead, which stood 95% full in 1999 with over 50 MAF in reserve. Over two decades, prolonged overdraft drew down roughly 37 MAF of stored water, leaving combined storage at just 25% of capacity by 2022.

4. Critical Reservoir Levels and Paid Conservation

Shortage Tiers & Conservation

Near-record low elevations prompted mandatory shortage cuts and temporary federal payments to keep reservoirs above dead pool.

By mid-2022, Lake Powell dropped to 3,522 ft, within 32 feet of minimum power pool (3,490 ft), where Glen Canyon Dam can no longer generate electricity. Water managers faced the risk of reservoirs falling toward dead pool (3,370 ft at Powell, 895 ft at Mead), where water can no longer pass through dam outlets by gravity.

Mandatory Lower Basin Shortage Cuts: The Bureau of Reclamation triggered Tier 1 and Tier 2 shortage reductions under the 2007 Interim Guidelines. Under its junior priority, Arizona absorbed cuts exceeding 590,000 AF—over 20% of its Colorado River supply—leading to extensive agricultural fallowing in Pinal County while senior California rights sustained zero mandatory cuts.
Compensated System Conservation (IRA Funding): To prevent storage from crashing before permanent rules could be written, the federal government committed $1.2 billion through the Inflation Reduction Act. Reclamation paid irrigation districts and tribal nations roughly $400 per acre-foot to temporarily leave 3.0 MAF of water in Lake Mead and Lake Powell through 2026.

5. Post-2026 Operating Rules

Post-2026 Framework

Current interim guidelines expire at the end of 2026, requiring basin states to tie future water use directly to actual river yields.

The 2007 Interim Guidelines, the 2019 Drought Contingency Plans, and short-term federal conservation payouts all expire on December 31, 2026. Because Lake Powell and Lake Mead no longer hold enough stored water to buffer multi-year deficits, the next set of operating rules must match water use to actual river flow rather than 1922 paper targets.

Under the post-2026 National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) review, the seven basin states, 30 Native American tribes, and the Bureau of Reclamation are negotiating replacement rules to reduce basin-wide consumptive use by 2.0 to 4.0 MAF annually. Key proposals under active negotiation include debiting Lower Basin users for 1.5 MAF of annual evaporation and transit losses, settling unquantified tribal water rights, and expanding municipal recycling and agricultural efficiency to establish a durable water budget.

1. USGS Streamflow Observations

USGS REST API

Real-time streamflow across the 25+ river reaches and confluences is ingested directly from the USGS Instantaneous Values (IV) Web Service. Measurements are refreshed at 15- to 60-minute intervals from acoustic Doppler and stage sensors.

ParameterValueTechnical Description
sites e.g. 09380000, 09070500 Comma-delimited 8-digit USGS station IDs (Lees Ferry, Dotsero, Cameo, Cisco, etc.).
parameterCd 00060 / 62614 00060 = Discharge in cubic feet per second (cfs); 62614 = Lake elevation (ft).
siteStatus all Includes active operational streamgage stations across all 7 basin states.
format json Structured WaterML 2.0 / JSON time-series output.
Python 3 • requests (Live Streamflow) 
import requests

# Base endpoint for USGS Instantaneous Values (IV)
url = "https://waterservices.usgs.gov/nwis/iv/"

# Query Sentinel Gauges across Upper and Lower Basins:
# 09380000 = Lees Ferry Compact Divide
# 09070500 = Dotsero (Upper Mainstem)
# 09180500 = Cisco (near UT-CO border)
# 09429100 = Below Palo Verde Dam (Lower Basin)
params = {
    "sites": "09380000,09070500,09180500,09429100",
    "parameterCd": "00060",  # Streamflow Discharge (cfs)
    "siteStatus": "all",
    "format": "json"
}

response = requests.get(url, params=params, timeout=12)
data = response.json()

print(f"{'USGS Gauge ID':<14} | {'Station Name':<38} | {'Discharge (cfs)':>15}")
print("-" * 72)

for ts in data["value"]["timeSeries"]:
    site_id = ts["sourceInfo"]["siteCode"][0]["value"]
    site_name = ts["sourceInfo"]["siteName"]
    readings = ts["values"][0]["value"]
    if readings:
        latest = readings[-1]
        cfs_val = float(latest["value"]) if latest["value"] != "-999999" else 0.0
        print(f"{site_id:<14} | {site_name[:38]:<38} | {cfs_val:>15,.1f}")

2. Colorado Trans-Mountain Diversions

Colorado DWR API

Trans-mountain diversions that carry West Slope water underneath the Continental Divide to Denver and Front Range cities are monitored via the Colorado Division of Water Resources (CDWR) HydroBase REST API v2. This tracks tunnels that remove water before it ever reaches the Lower Basin.

Endpoint / ParamValueTechnical Description
telemetrytimeserieshour Hourly Readings Active hourly discharge for diversion tunnels (ROBTUNCO, MOFTUNCO, ADATUNCO, BOUTUNCO).
telemetrytimeseriesday Daily Accounting Daily storage volume and elevation for Front Range municipal reservoirs (GRARESCO, DILRESCO, GRMRESCO).
abbrev Structure Codes Comma-separated unique CDWR structure identifiers.
Python 3 • requests (Trans-Mountain Tunnels) 
import requests

# Colorado Division of Water Resources (HydroBase REST API v2)
# Hourly telemetric discharge for Continental Divide diversion tunnels:
# ROBTUNCO = Harold D. Roberts Tunnel (Dillon -> South Platte / Denver)
# MOFTUNCO = Moffat Water Tunnel (Fraser -> Gross Res / Denver)
# ADATUNCO = Alva B. Adams Tunnel (Grand Lake -> Estes Park / Northern)
# BOUTUNCO = Charles H. Boustead Tunnel (Fryingpan -> Arkansas / Aurora)

base_url = "https://dwr.state.co.us/Rest/GET/api/v2/telemetrystations/telemetrytimeserieshour/"
params = {
    "abbrev": "ROBTUNCO,MOFTUNCO,ADATUNCO,BOUTUNCO",
    "format": "json"
}

headers = {"User-Agent": "DAT1001-Research/1.0"}
res = requests.get(base_url, params=params, headers=headers, timeout=10).json()

# Deduplicate to extract the latest reading per tunnel
tunnel_flows = {}
for item in res.get("ResultList", []):
    if item.get("parameter") == "DISCHRG":
        code = item["abbrev"]
        val = max(0.0, float(item.get("measValue", 0.0)))
        dt = item.get("measDateTime")
        if code not in tunnel_flows or dt > tunnel_flows[code]["time"]:
            tunnel_flows[code] = {"flow_cfs": val, "time": dt, "name": item["stationName"]}

print(f"{'Tunnel Code':<10} | {'Infrastructure Name':<32} | {'Export (cfs)':>12}")
print("-" * 60)
for code, info in tunnel_flows.items():
    print(f"{code:<10} | {info['name'][:32]:<32} | {info['flow_cfs']:>12,.1f}")

3. Reclamation Reservoir Operations

USBR HydroData

Major federal storage reservoirs—Lake Powell (Glen Canyon Dam), Lake Mead (Hoover Dam), Flaming Gorge, Navajo, and Blue Mesa—are tracked using dual feeds: the Upper Colorado Region HydroData CSV API and the Lower Colorado Hourly Operations teletype log.

Provider ServiceIdentifierRole / Field
UC HydroData CSV Res 919 (Powell), 917 (Flaming Gorge) Param 17.csv = Storage in Acre-Feet; Param 49.csv = Water surface elevation (ft).
LC Hourly Operations /riverops/hourly7.html Live hourly feed for Hoover, Davis, and Parker Dam releases and Mead/Mohave/Havasu elevations.

import pandas as pd
import requests
import re

# 1. USBR Upper Colorado Region HydroData CSV API
# Reservoir IDs: 919 = Lake Powell, 917 = Flaming Gorge, 920 = Navajo
# Parameter 17 = Storage (Acre-Feet), Parameter 49 = Elevation (Feet)
powell_csv_url = "https://www.usbr.gov/uc/water/hydrodata/reservoir_data/919/csv/17.csv"

df_powell = pd.read_csv(powell_csv_url, skiprows=1, names=["datetime", "storage_af"])
latest_powell = df_powell.dropna().iloc[-1]
print(f"Lake Powell Storage: {float(latest_powell['storage_af']):,.0f} AF (as of {latest_powell['datetime']})")

# 2. USBR Lower Colorado Region Teletype Feed (Hourly Operations)
# Reports Hoover Dam / Lake Mead, Davis Dam / Mohave, and Parker Dam / Havasu
hourly_url = "https://www.usbr.gov/lc/region/g4000/hourly/hourly.html"
txt = requests.get(hourly_url, timeout=10).text

# Parse teletype table format: [HOUR-RANGE] [MEAD_EL] [HOOVER_CFS] [MOHAVE_EL] ...
pattern = r'^\s*(\d{4}-\d{4})\s+([\d\.]+)\s+(\d+)\s+([\d\.]+)\s+([\d\.]+)\s+(\d+)\s+([\d\.]+)\s+([\d\.]+)'
for line in txt.splitlines():
    match = re.match(pattern, line)
    if match:
        time_slot, mead_el, hoover_cfs, mohave_el, mohave_af, davis_cfs, havasu_el, havasu_af = match.groups()
        print(f"Lake Mead Elevation: {mead_el} ft | Hoover Dam Release: {int(hoover_cfs):,} cfs ({time_slot})")

4. River Reach Mass-Balance Pipeline

Data Architecture

All data sources are synthesized by an automated Python pipeline (src/fetch_water_data.py and src/generate_river_network.py). To ensure physical realism across unmonitored river segments, the pipeline enforces reach mass balance:

Reach Mass Balance: Downstream Flow = Upstream Inflow + Tributary Inflows − Trans-Mountain Exports − Canal Diversions ± Reservoir Storage Change

Python 3 • Multi-Threaded Ingestion & GeoJSON Export

    streamflow = future_usgs.result()
