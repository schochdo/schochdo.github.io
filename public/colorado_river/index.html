Act 1 • The Foundational Dilemma

The Over-Allocation Gap: Statutory Paper Rights vs. 21st-Century Physical Flow

The fundamental management challenge of the Colorado River Basin is mathematical over-allocation. The 1922 Colorado River Compact and subsequent statutory agreements divided 17.5 Million Acre-Feet per year (MAF/yr) of consumptive beneficial use rights based on an anomalously wet pluvial decade (1905–1922). Crucially, the treaties omitted reservoir evaporation and transit seepage (an additional ~1.8 MAF/yr). Against modern 21st-century physical inflows (12.4 MAF/yr, 2000–2026), total baseline commitments exceed physical supply by ~5.1 MAF/yr. Downstream demands have exceeded inflows for a quarter century, a structural overdraft absorbed by draining Lake Mead and Lake Powell from near-full (56.2 MAF in 1999) to critical storage (~17.7 MAF / 29.5%) today.

Act 3 • The Multi-Decade Reckoning

Buffer Exhaustion: How 25 Years of Deficit Drained 60 Million Acre-Feet

To survive natural drought and seasonal mismatches, the United States built ~60 MAF of storage capacity—the largest reservoir system in North America. Lake Mead (Hoover Dam), Lake Powell (Glen Canyon Dam), and Upper CRSP reservoirs (Flaming Gorge, Navajo, Blue Mesa) hold 4 years of average river flow. Total storage peaked at 56.2 MAF (94% full) in 1984. But for 25 straight years, downstream demands have exceeded physical inflows. Draining storage to cover the 5.1 MAF annual deficit has exhausted the buffer to ~17.7 MAF (29.5%) today.

The Mining Law Paradox: "Use It or Lose It" & Downstream Seniority

Prior Appropriation

Western water law originated in 19th-century California gold rush mining camps rather than English riparian sharing. This legal architecture created a structural dilemma for modern river management:

1. Senior Rights Sit at the Bottom of the River Zero Baseline Cuts

The Imperial Irrigation District holds 1901 Present Perfected Rights (2.6+ MAF/yr senior entitlement) predating the 1922 Compact. Because these rights sit at California's southern border, the entire river must bypass all upstream states to fulfill them first.

2. The "Use It or Lose It" Forfeiture Trap Statutory Forfeiture

Under western water law, unexercised paper allocations risk statutory forfeiture/abandonment. This penalizes conservation and creates a perverse legal incentive to flood-irrigate low-value forage crops (alfalfa) in 115°F desert heat to defend paper entitlements.

3. Upstream Arizona Cities Absorb First Cuts 1968 Basin Project Act

To secure California's congressional votes for funding the Central Arizona Project (CAP), Arizona agreed that CAP would remain junior to California. When shortage triggers hit Lake Mead, Phoenix and Tucson cities take mandatory cuts before California farms take any.

4. The December 2026 Operating Cliff Expires Dec 31, 2026

The 2007 Interim Guidelines and 2019 DCP expire at the end of 2026. The seven basin states are in federal negotiations to permanently bridge the 2 to 4 MAF/yr deficit before federal courts intervene.

Key Finding:

Over 80% of diverted water goes to desert agriculture. Because California holds 1901 senior perfected rights backed by "use it or lose it" forfeiture rules, growers are legally disincentivized from conserving, while junior upstream cities in Arizona face mandatory cuts. When operating rules expire in December 2026, the basin must rewrite a century of water law to align legal allocations with physical hydrology.

Legal & Historical Foundation • 1922–2026 Statutory Framework

The Law of the River: Paper Water vs. Wet Water

How the 1922 Compact's overestimate of river flow, the senior priority system, and two decades of reservoir drawdowns led to the post-2026 negotiations.

Jump To: 1. 1922 Baseline • 2. Seniority & Cuts • 3. Reservoir Drawdown • 4. Low-Storage Triggers • 5. Post-2026 Rules

1. The 1922 Compact Baseline

Historic Foundation

Negotiators assumed the river produced over 17 MAF per year, creating an immediate gap between legal promises and physical supply.

Delegates from the seven basin states and Herbert Hoover signed the 1922 Colorado River Compact in Santa Fe, New Mexico, splitting the watershed at Lees Ferry, Arizona. The compact apportioned 7.5 million acre-feet (MAF) per year to the Upper Basin and 7.5 MAF per year to the Lower Basin in perpetuity. In 1944, the Mexican Water Treaty committed another 1.5 MAF per year at Morelos Dam, bringing statutory paper water promises to 16.5 MAF per year without setting aside water for reservoir evaporation.

Baseline Hydrology: Paper Allocations vs. Physical Wet Water

Negotiators based their allocations on gage records from the unusually wet period between 1905 and 1922, estimating average annual river runoff above 17 MAF. Early USGS field warnings from hydrologist E.C. LaRue showed that long-term flows were significantly lower, but commissioners prioritized state unanimity over hydrology. A century of USGS records and tree-ring data show that natural inflow averages closer to 12.5 to 13.2 MAF per year. Because reservoir evaporation and transit losses consume another 1.5 to 1.8 MAF per year, statutory paper allocations exceed physical wet water by roughly 4 to 5 MAF annually.

2. Senior Rights and Priority Cuts

Legal Hierarchy

The doctrine of prior appropriation protects older agricultural claims while junior municipal canals take early cuts during shortages.

Western water administration follows the doctrine of prior appropriation: "first in time, first in right." The user who first put river water to beneficial use holds senior legal priority and receives a full allocation before newer rights receive water. In the Colorado River Basin, this rule created an unequal priority structure that determines which states absorb shortages first:

Senior Present Perfected Rights in California (1901): Southern California agricultural users—led by the Imperial Irrigation District (IID) and Coachella Valley Water District—hold senior water rights established in 1901 that pre-date the 1922 Compact. Confirmed by the Supreme Court in Arizona v. California (1963), IID's 3.1 MAF entitlement is senior to all municipal water projects across the Lower Basin.
Subordination of the Central Arizona Project (1968): To obtain federal authorization and funding for the 336-mile Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal in the 1968 Basin Project Act, Arizona agreed that CAP deliveries are junior to California's 4.4 MAF baseline allocation. As a result, when shortage triggers occur, Arizona takes the first and deepest cuts while senior California agricultural users receive their full supply.
Upper Basin Headwater Administration: Without large downstream regulatory reservoirs above their diversion points, Upper Basin users divert water directly from mountain streams based on local priority calls. Senior calls at headwater facilities regularly curtail junior Front Range trans-mountain diversions during late-summer low flows. Because of these physical and legal limits, Upper Basin consumptive use averages 4.0 to 4.5 MAF per year, well below its 7.5 MAF paper apportionment.

3. Draining Storage to Cover the Deficit

Storage Depletion

The basin covered a multi-year gap between high demand and falling snowmelt by drawing down 37 MAF of stored reservoir water.

Even when Upper Basin snowmelt dropped after 2000, Lower Basin users continued drawing full allocations while reservoir evaporation and transit losses consumed roughly 1.5 MAF per year. Instead of reducing water use to match lower snowmelt, the basin operated at an annual structural deficit of 2.0 to 3.5 MAF per year.

The basin bridged this multi-year deficit by draining Lake Powell and Lake Mead, which stood 95% full in 1999 with over 50 MAF in reserve. Over two decades, prolonged overdraft drew down roughly 37 MAF of stored water, leaving combined storage at just 25% of capacity by 2022.

4. Critical Reservoir Levels and Paid Conservation

Shortage Tiers & Conservation

Near-record low elevations prompted mandatory shortage cuts and temporary federal payments to keep reservoirs above dead pool.

By mid-2022, Lake Powell dropped to 3,522 ft, within 32 feet of minimum power pool (3,490 ft), where Glen Canyon Dam can no longer generate electricity. Water managers faced the risk of reservoirs falling toward dead pool (3,370 ft at Powell, 895 ft at Mead), where water can no longer pass through dam outlets by gravity.

Mandatory Lower Basin Shortage Cuts: The Bureau of Reclamation triggered Tier 1 and Tier 2 shortage reductions under the 2007 Interim Guidelines. Under its junior priority, Arizona absorbed cuts exceeding 590,000 AF—over 20% of its Colorado River supply—leading to extensive agricultural fallowing in Pinal County while senior California rights sustained zero mandatory cuts.
Compensated System Conservation (IRA Funding): To prevent storage from crashing before permanent rules could be written, the federal government committed $1.2 billion through the Inflation Reduction Act. Reclamation paid irrigation districts and tribal nations roughly $400 per acre-foot to temporarily leave 3.0 MAF of water in Lake Mead and Lake Powell through 2026.

5. Post-2026 Operating Rules

Post-2026 Framework

Current interim guidelines expire at the end of 2026, requiring basin states to tie future water use directly to actual river yields.

The 2007 Interim Guidelines, the 2019 Drought Contingency Plans, and short-term federal conservation payouts all expire on December 31, 2026. Because Lake Powell and Lake Mead no longer hold enough stored water to buffer multi-year deficits, the next set of operating rules must match water use to actual river flow rather than 1922 paper targets.

Under the post-2026 National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) review, the seven basin states, 30 Native American tribes, and the Bureau of Reclamation are negotiating replacement rules to reduce basin-wide consumptive use by 2.0 to 4.0 MAF annually. Key proposals under active negotiation include debiting Lower Basin users for 1.5 MAF of annual evaporation and transit losses, settling unquantified tribal water rights, and expanding municipal recycling and agricultural efficiency to establish a durable water budget.

3. Notebook Execution and Distribution

How research notebooks are rendered, distributed, and executed across web and cloud environments

Option A: Static HTML Web Hosting (Current Implementation)

The portal pre-renders every Jupyter notebook into standalone, zero-dependency HTML files inside web/notebooks/ using Pygments and KaTeX. This requires zero Python or Jupyter installation for portal visitors, loads instantly on mobile/desktop, and can be hosted statically on GitHub Pages, Netlify, or any web server.

Option B: Google Colab (Zero-Setup Interactive Execution)

Researchers and students can open any .ipynb notebook directly in Google Colab with 1 click. Colab runs code in a free Google Cloud sandbox, allowing users to rerun live USGS API queries, retrain K-Means clusters, or test new lag parameters without configuring a local Python environment.

Option C: Project Jupyter nbviewer (Official Jupyter Service)

Project Jupyter maintains nbviewer.org ↗, an official, free cloud service. By entering the public GitHub repository URL, nbviewer dynamically compiles notebooks on demand with mathematical KaTeX formulas and high-resolution figures.

Option D: MyBinder (Interactive Cloud JupyterLab)

mybinder.org ↗ builds reproducible Docker containers directly from a GitHub repository's environment.yml or requirements.txt, launching a full interactive JupyterLab environment in the user's browser.
