1. The 1922 Compact Baseline

Historic Foundation

Negotiators assumed the river produced over 17 MAF per year, creating an immediate gap between legal promises and physical supply.

Delegates from the seven basin states and Herbert Hoover signed the 1922 Colorado River Compact in Santa Fe, New Mexico, splitting the watershed at Lees Ferry, Arizona. The compact apportioned 7.5 million acre-feet (MAF) per year to the Upper Basin and 7.5 MAF per year to the Lower Basin in perpetuity (Art. III.a), plus authorized an additional 1.0 MAF of Lower Basin consumptive use (Art. III.b). In 1944, the Mexican Water Treaty committed another 1.5 MAF per year at Morelos Dam (Art. 10), bringing statutory paper water apportionments to 17.5 MAF per year—without setting aside water for measured mainstem reservoir evaporation (1.32 MAF/yr across 2000–2024, totaling 18.82 MAF/yr on the mainstem).

Baseline Hydrology: Paper Allocations vs. Physical Wet Water

Negotiators based their allocations on gage records from the unusually wet period between 1905 and 1922, estimating average annual river runoff above 17 MAF. Early USGS field warnings from hydrologist E.C. LaRue showed that long-term flows were significantly lower, but commissioners prioritized state unanimity over hydrology. Over the 25-year 2000–2024 Millennium Drought baseline (ending in 2024 due to the 1–2 year federal verification lag on post-season Consumptive Uses & Natural Flow workbooks; live gages run through Nov 2026), natural flow averaged 12.40 MAF/yr at Lees Ferry (12.76 MAF/yr including 0.36 MAF/yr from Lower Basin mainstem tributaries). Combined with 1.32 MAF/yr in measured mainstem reservoir evaporation, total paper claims + evaporation reach 18.82 MAF/yr, creating a +6.06 MAF/yr structural over-allocation (+4.74 MAF/yr against statutory-only claims).

2. Senior Rights and Priority Cuts

Legal Hierarchy

The doctrine of prior appropriation protects older agricultural claims while junior municipal canals take early cuts during shortages.

Western water administration follows the doctrine of prior appropriation: "first in time, first in right." The user who first put river water to beneficial use holds senior legal priority and receives a full allocation before newer rights receive water. In the Colorado River Basin, this rule created an unequal priority structure that determines which states absorb shortages first:

Senior Present Perfected Rights in California (1901): Southern California agricultural users—led by the Imperial Irrigation District (IID) and Coachella Valley Water District—hold 1901 water rights that predate the 1922 Compact. Confirmed by the Supreme Court in Arizona v. California (1963), IID's 3.1 MAF entitlement is senior to all Lower Basin municipal water projects.
The Shoshone Call & Upper Basin Headwaters (1902): In Glenwood Canyon, the Shoshone Hydroelectric Plant holds a non-consumptive 1902 senior right for 1,250 cfs (administered at USGS #09070500 near Dotsero) that predates Colorado's major Front Range trans-mountain tunnels. During low flows, an active Shoshone Call compels upstream tunnels to bypass water down the West Slope mainstem; combined with other headwater priority limits, Upper Basin state consumptive use averaged 3.90 MAF/yr across 2000–2024 (4.37 MAF/yr including CRSP mainstem evaporation)—well below its 7.50 MAF paper apportionment.
Subordination of the Central Arizona Project (1968): To obtain federal authorization and funding for the 336-mile Central Arizona Project (CAP) canal in the 1968 Basin Project Act, Arizona agreed that CAP deliveries are junior to California's 4.4 MAF baseline allocation. When shortage triggers occur, Arizona absorbs the first and deepest cuts while senior California agricultural users receive their full supply.

3. Draining Storage to Cover the Deficit

Storage Depletion

Over the 2000–2024 baseline, 5-reservoir storage dropped 29.8 MAF net (-1.19 MAF/yr), reaching a 37.2 MAF peak-to-trough low in March 2023.

Even as Upper Basin natural runoff dropped sharply after 1999, Lower Basin users continued drawing nearly full entitlements while mainstem reservoir evaporation consumed 1.32 MAF/yr (2000–2024 USBR CUL average). Between Dec 31, 1999 (51.8 MAF) and Dec 31, 2024 (22.0 MAF), net storage across the five major federal reservoirs fell by 29.8 MAF (-1.19 MAF/yr: Mead -0.65, Powell -0.51, CRSP -0.03 MAF/yr), closing the 25-year physical water budget.

Measured from seasonal peak to intra-period trough before the wet 2023 snowpack rebound, the five major federal reservoirs fell from 53.5 MAF in September 1999 (Powell + Mead 47.7 MAF, 94.5% full) to 16.3 MAF on March 14, 2023 (Powell + Mead 12.7 MAF, 25.1% full)—representing 37.2 MAF of peak-to-trough depletion.

4. Critical Reservoir Levels and Paid Conservation

Shortage Tiers & Conservation

Near-record low elevations prompted mandatory shortage cuts and temporary federal payments to keep reservoirs above dead pool.

By mid-2022, Lake Powell dropped to 3,522 ft, within 32 feet of minimum power pool (3,490 ft), where Glen Canyon Dam can no longer generate electricity. Water managers faced the risk of reservoirs falling toward dead pool (3,370 ft at Powell, 895 ft at Mead), where water can no longer pass through dam outlets by gravity.

Mandatory Lower Basin Shortage Cuts: The Bureau of Reclamation triggered Tier 1 and Tier 2 shortage reductions under the 2007 Interim Guidelines. Under its junior priority, Arizona absorbed cuts exceeding 590,000 AF—over 20% of its Colorado River supply—leading to extensive agricultural fallowing in Pinal County while senior California rights sustained zero mandatory cuts.
Compensated System Conservation (IRA Funding): To prevent storage from crashing before permanent rules could be written, the federal government committed $1.2 billion through the Inflation Reduction Act. Reclamation paid irrigation districts and tribal nations roughly $400 per acre-foot to temporarily leave 3.0 MAF of water in Lake Mead and Lake Powell through 2026.

5. Post-2026 Operating Rules

Post-2026 Framework

Current interim guidelines expire at the end of 2026, requiring basin states to tie future water use directly to actual river yields.

The 2007 Interim Guidelines, the 2019 Drought Contingency Plans, and short-term federal conservation payouts all expire on December 31, 2026. Because Lake Powell and Lake Mead no longer hold enough stored water to buffer multi-year deficits, the next set of operating rules must match water use to actual river flow rather than 1922 paper targets.

Under the post-2026 National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA) review, the seven basin states, 30 Native American tribes, and the Bureau of Reclamation are negotiating replacement rules to reduce basin-wide consumptive use by 2.0 to 4.0 MAF annually. Key proposals under active negotiation include debiting Lower Basin users for 1.5 MAF of annual evaporation and transit losses, settling unquantified tribal water rights, and expanding municipal recycling and agricultural efficiency to establish a durable water budget.

Plain-English reference for every legal, agency, infrastructure, and hydrologic acronym used in this project.

Water Law & Accounting

CUL
—
Consumptive Uses and Losses: USBR's official annual accounting report of water consumed by states, agriculture, cities, and reservoir evaporation.
BCPA
—
Boulder Canyon Project Act (1928): Authorized Hoover Dam and divided the Lower Basin's 7.5 MAF (CA 4.4, AZ 2.8, NV 0.3).
CRSP
—
Colorado River Storage Project (1956): Authorized Glen Canyon/Lake Powell, Flaming Gorge, Navajo, and Blue Mesa (Aspinall Unit).
DROA
—
Drought Response Operations Agreement (2019): Authorizes emergency releases from Flaming Gorge and Blue Mesa to defend Lake Powell's 3,490 ft minimum power pool.
DCP
—
Drought Contingency Plan (2019): Upper and Lower Basin voluntary shortage-sharing agreements.

Hydrology & Units

cfs
—
Cubic feet per second: Instantaneous flow rate (1 cfs sustained for 1 year ≈ 724 AF).
AF / MAF
—
Acre-Feet / Million Acre-Feet: Cumulative water volume (1 AF = 1 acre covered 1 ft deep ≈ 325,851 gallons).
WY
—
Water Year: Oct 1 – Sep 30, named for the calendar year in which it ends (WY 2024 = Oct 2023 – Sep 2024).
RBI
—
Richards–Baker Flashiness Index: Measures day-to-day streamflow volatility (low = snowmelt/dams, high = desert flash floods).
POR
—
Period of Record: The full multi-decade span of years a gage has been operating.
EOM
—
End of Month: End-of-month reservoir elevation or storage accounting.
NGVD29
—
National Geodetic Vertical Datum of 1929: Legacy vertical elevation datum USBR uses for Lake Mead and Lake Powell pool levels.
